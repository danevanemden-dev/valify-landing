Your gain gets split in two. How it's split is worth checking.

The new rules start on 1 July 2027. For a property bought before that date and sold after it, the gain is divided at the changeover. The earlier part keeps the 50% CGT discount; the later part moves to cost base indexation, with a minimum tax rate of 30%. The more of your gain that sits on the earlier side, the better the outcome.

Two ways to draw the line

Treasury's draft formula

It works on the assumption that your property grew at one steady rate for every year you've owned it. Markets rarely move that evenly, so where yours didn't, a meaningful share of the gain can land on a different side of the line than it really belongs, sometimes to your cost and sometimes to your benefit.

An independent valuation

An accredited valuer fixes a defensible, point-in-time market value at the changeover date. You can hold it up against the formula's result and use whichever one better reflects your circumstances.

Timing of growth is what usually decides which is better. If most of your property's growth is still ahead of you, the formula can end up working in your favour, because it treats more of the gain as though it happened earlier, under the 50% discount. If most of the growth came before July 2027, it can do the opposite, pushing more of the gain into the higher-taxed period than actually belongs there, and a valuation may do better. Your purchase price plays a part too: a higher original price tends to make the formula's result more favourable, whenever the growth occurred.
